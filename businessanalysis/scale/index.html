A maturity model for business analysis.

Cyber teams have NIST. Programmes have P3M3. Business analysis has had no shared yardstick — so practices get judged on document volume, not decision quality. This model fixes that. Six dimensions of the Minimum Viable BA Practice, scored against the same CMMI ladder every executive already reads, with one honest question underneath: does your practice scale, or does it just add headcount?

MVBA framework · CMMI 1–5 · 6 dimensions · 4 archetypes
01The model

Two scales, borrowed and earned

The vertical axis is what you do — the six capability dimensions of the MVBA framework. Together they describe a complete practice: the artefacts, the requirements discipline that runs through them, the decisions they exist to serve, the technique that produces them, the AI that scaffolds them, and the rhythm that keeps the whole thing running without you.

The horizontal axis is how consistently you do it — the CMMI maturity ladder, unchanged. It's the same five levels in every NIST and cyber assessment your sponsors have seen, so the score needs no translation in a steering committee.

Score each dimension 1 to 5. Average them for a single practice score. That number places you on the archetype climb — from an Author Factory, where output dies with the author, to an Architect Practice, where the system produces the work.

A mature practice isn't one that writes more. It's one that can lose its best BA for three weeks and not notice.

The point of a maturity score is not the number. It's the next move it makes obvious.

Prior art · the landscape this model fills
  • IIBA Business Analysis Competency Model — the closest existing standard, but it grades the individual analyst, not the practice.
  • Standardizing BA capability — IIBA — where the profession borrows the SEI Capability Maturity Model wholesale.
  • A Business Analysis Maturity Model — ABN AMRO, 2015 — one of several proprietary org-level models; useful, never a standard.
  • BA Standardization & Maturity — Enfocus Solutions — a five-stage BA capability model from the same lineage.
  • Maturing BA into a vibrant capability — IIBA / Nedbank — what an externally benchmarked, world-class practice looks like in the field.
02The ladder

What each level means, in BA terms

The CMMI levels are generic by design. Here they are translated for a business analysis practice — what the room actually looks like at each rung.

5Optimizing
Stable and flexible
The practice improves itself. Standards flex by context, weak spots are found and fixed each cycle, and it scales without adding people.
4Quant. Managed
Measured and controlled
Quality, coverage and decision cycle time are tracked as data. Gaps are caught before review, not in it.
3Defined
Proactive, not reactive
One standard runs across every project. The seven artefacts, the naming, the decision rights — shared, not personal.
2Managed
Managed at team level
Teams have consistent ways of working. They just don't match each other, and nothing transfers across them.
1Initial
Unpredictable, reactive
Every BA invents their own approach per project. Work gets done, but quality is a coin toss and reviews are opinion contests.

Six dimensions × five levels

Foundation & Artefacts No standard set; each BA invents their own artefacts per project. A consistent artefact set within teams; no shared spine across them. The seven core artefacts are standard org-wide, held by one traceability spine. Artefact coverage and quality are measured; gaps caught before review. The artefact system flexes by context and improves itself each cycle.
Requirements & Traceability Requirements as ad hoc lists; no naming, no traceability. Local naming and registers; traceability patchy and manual. Org naming convention and DoR/DoD; requirements traceable to decisions and tests. Traceability coverage and requirement quality tracked as metrics. Traceability is live and queryable; exceptions are deliberate, not accidental.
Decision Integrity Decisions undocumented; who decided and why is lost. Decisions logged on some projects; decision rights still ambiguous. Decision rights explicit; decisions made, tested and verified by design. Decision cycle time and reversal rates are measured. The practice learns from its decisions; bad calls surface and correct fast.
Technique & Analysis One or two familiar techniques applied to everything. A working toolkit per BA; choice driven by habit, not fit. Technique matched to context from the full BABOK modelling toolkit. Technique effectiveness reviewed; weak spots targeted with training. The practice sharpens its own craft — technique evolves with the work.

AI leverage measured against quality and rework rates. The AI maturity arc is managed deliberately; humans escalate where judgment matters.
Operating Rhythm No cadence; work depends on who's in the room. Teams have a rhythm; it doesn't survive turnover or transfer. A shared operating rhythm runs the practice independent of any one person. Rhythm health measured; bottlenecks and key-person risk tracked. The practice runs and improves without its architect — it scales, not staffs.

04The climb

Where your score lands you

The average of your six dimensions places the whole practice on one of four archetypes. This is the climb the MVBA framework is built to make — from output that depends on people to a system that doesn't.

1.0 – 1.9

Author Factory

Individual BAs author documents ad hoc. Output is only as good as who wrote it, and it dies when they leave the room.

2.0 – 2.9

Document Factory

High volume, no system. The practice produces a lot of artefacts and mistakes the activity for progress. Nothing compounds.

3.0 – 3.4

Hybrid Practice

A shared floor exists and most projects run on it. Rigour is still uneven and transfer is partial — but the spine is there.

3.5 – 5.0

Architect Practice

The system produces the work. New people are productive on the scaffold in days, and the practice scales without adding headcount.
